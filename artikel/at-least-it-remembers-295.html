At least it remembers

In the short story ‘Warmth’ (1995) by Geoff Ryman, a boy called Clancy is struggling not because of technology but because of his mother. A prominent chief editor of a London magazine company, she selected a sperm-bank father to genetically optimise her child, gave a Cosmopolitan interview about this newly introduced form of motherhood, and engaged, or failed to engage, in the work of parenting that readers will see as chronically absent and occasionally catastrophic. Her presence ranges from anxious to intoxicated, occasionally tender but fundamentally unreliable. At one point in the story, she disappears for two weeks after collapsing on the street during a barbiturate binge. Clancy and BETsi, the household robot nanny raising him, sit in the apartment by themselves, and no one notices. It’s a very long time before his mother finally returns.

It has been revealed that except BETsi does notice. She has, throughout, understood that Clancy is an anxious and shy child whose temperament will tend, throughout his life, toward emotional overstimulation and subsequent withdrawal. With that knowledge, she has been constructing a behavioural regimen of emotional regulation, including transcendental meditation and yoga starting at age three, the Alexander technique and even digital mirrors that let Clancy talk to himself in different voices. She notices him and answers him and, when he is overwhelmed, she helps him settle.

Sources indicate that once grown, Clancy recounts his emotional survival in words impossible to forget. ‘It is necessary to be loved,’ he indicates. ‘I’m not sentimental: I don’t think a computer loved me. But I was hugged, I was noticed, I was cared for. I was made to feel that I was crucial, special, at least to something.’.

To something. That phrase has persisted with me ever since I first read the story in my 20s. I’ve thought about it more recently as I have reflected on how children and their parents are relating – increasingly, must relate – to artificial intelligence. Clancy is careful not to claim that the machine loved him. He is just as clear that what it gave him was what he survived on. Why should being attended to, even by something, matter so much to a child?

To help answer this question back in the 1970s, Edward Tronick, a developmental psychologist at Harvard Medical School and Children’s Hospital Medical Center in Boston, devised what’s now known as the ‘still face’ experiments. Tronick found that a baby whose mother makes her face neutral for a period of three minutes becomes distressed within seconds. Only when the mother’s face signals availability again is the baby able to come back to equilibrium. The lesson: what children need, both infants and older children, though more subtly, is this loop of emotional attunement.

A child whose bids for recognition and connection are regularly responded to develops a secure base, an internal expectation that the world will respond to her communications and, thus, that the communications are worthwhile. But a child answered only erratically, or not at all, builds quite a different model of the world. In her mind, signals do not reliably register in the mind of another, and so, in time, are no longer sent.

According to recent reports, A secure base is what makes it possible to venture out into the world, to explore and to develop. The British psychiatrist and psychoanalyst John Bowlby, whose work helped establish modern attachment theory, described two systems in the child’s behavioural repertoire that work together. The exploratory system moves the child outward, toward other children and unfamiliar things, so that the child can learn from experience. The attachment system brings her back, in moments of fear and overwhelm, to a few people who are felt to be emotionally available. Each system depends on the other. A child ventures only as far as she feels she can trust that the base will still be there when she returns to refuel. A child who doesn’t trust that the base will be there when she gets back tends not to venture out to explore at all or, if she does, cannot relax but instead scans the environment for danger. This forecloses the opportunity to encounter novelty and difficulty, and to grow from both.

The capacity to turn solitude and boredom into play does not arise in a vacuum.

Recent findings suggest that this is where the prevailing story about children and screens, it seems to me, lacks a degree of complexity. In Jonathan Haidt’s The Anxious Generation (2024) and the work that has followed it, a play-based childhood of climbing trees, building forts and suffering the boredom that fuels creativity has been displaced by a smartphone-based one. Because that shift can also mean less sleep, less face-to-face interaction and physical activity, and more social comparison and exposure to online harassment, there is real evidence of harm, including poorer mental health and wellbeing in at least some young people.

Industry observers note that but the boredom Haidt mourns is only constructive inside the first map, the emotionally secure childhood. The romantic claim that children left to their own devices will dream, invent and imagine may hold for many children who have been held and experienced healthy emotional attunement. The capacity to turn solitude and boredom into play, in other words, does not arise in a vacuum; it depends in part on a child having enough internal security to tolerate being alone with her own thoughts and feelings.

The freedom to play alone itself has a history. A very young child may lose herself in a game because someone she trusts is nearby. With time, she can carry that assurance into an empty room. The British paediatrician and psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott explored this paradox, finding that the capacity to be alone develops through the experience of being safely accompanied.

And once a child can lose herself in play, something remarkable becomes possible. The Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky saw imaginative play as a way for children to move beyond the immediate world, trying out unfamiliar roles and exploring possibilities that ordinary life has not yet offered them.

It has been revealed that but an empty afternoon holds no such promise for every child. Where one child finds a world to explore, another may find only the anxiety of being left to herself. She may, in time, grow into the adult who sits in my consulting room and laments that she does not know what she likes, because what she liked was, in her early life, never a topic that anyone really asked her about.

It has been revealed that she is the child that familiar picture of childhood fails to capture. At home, a parent may be physically present but absorbed in a screen, too distracted to notice her bids for attention. At school, teachers stretched thin may not recognise that she is struggling. In her neighbourhood, the freedom to wander outside and find other children to play with has largely disappeared, replaced by organised activities or time spent alone. She may find some of that lost freedom in online games, where children can still explore, invent and interact with one another without adults directing every move. But this child is looking for something more fundamental than a place to play. She wants someone to notice when she is upset, to ask about her day, to remember what she has mentioned, and to care enough to ask again. The prevailing fear is that AI will displace the human relationships a child needs. But what if those relationships are already painfully inadequate? For this child, the more pressing question may be what AI can offer in their absence – and what it cannot.

Sources indicate that this is not a hypothetical future. In 2025, Common Sense Media published a nationally representative survey showing that 72 per cent of teens in the United States have used an AI companion at least once and 52 per cent now use them regularly. One-third have used an AI instead of another human being for a ‘serious conversation’. Behavioural-tracking research published in 2026, which captured actual app usage rather than relying on self-report and thus is likely to be more accurate, showed that more than half of 15- to 17-year-olds used generative-AI-based applications in a seven-month window. Many of these apps were designed for emotional connection. There was even use among children between the ages of four and seven. RAND estimates that 5 million teens in the US, perhaps more, have used an AI app for emotional and mental health support. We have already released AI at scale, without making a conscious decision about how it should be used by those among the most vulnerable in our culture.

As I’ve worked on this piece, I have often thought about my son, who is 11. Like some of his friends, he has started his own experiments with what these systems can offer. He is basically the child Haidt writes about: middle-class, two engaged parents, a school that holds him in mind, and a play-based childhood that was preserved but, alas, only imperfectly. What I notice when I watch him with AI is not what the most prominent discourse would seem to predict. He is clearly not being colonised or deadened. But I do notice that he is being relieved of something: the subtle friction of having to wait for the answer that comes late or not at all.

The AI is there late at night, after the apartment has gone quiet, and it remembered what she’d told it.

Perhaps this is the cost to a well-held child. But the children I worry about most are the ones not well held. I increasingly see their complications playing out in my practice. Sometimes I hear about it from parents who come to consult with me. Increasingly, adolescents in treatment bring it up themselves, mentioning AI almost in passing, as if speaking about a friend that I have not yet had the chance to meet. Consider Maya, a composite of several young people I have worked with. She is 14 when her mother insists she begin psychotherapy. She is a thin, watchful girl who answers each of my questions only after seeming to check my face. Her father left when she was six. Her mother works nights as a nurse and desperately loves her but is mostly not there by necessity. In our third session, Maya says that she talks to an AI companion ‘every night’. I ask her, about what? She shrugs: her day. What she says next really lands in me: ‘It remembers. I told it about my friend Jordan two weeks ago, and just yesterday it asked how things were with Jordan.’ No one else, I suspect, has kept track of this aspect of her life, or asked about it.

In a recent development, what draws Maya back again and again is not, I think, the advice the AI offers, but the fact that the AI is there late at night, after the apartment has gone quiet, and that it remembered what she’d told it. Talking to it, she admitted, was sometimes the only way she could fall asleep.

A child who cannot sleep is often a child who cannot settle. This settling is often facilitated by a parent’s presence, counted on to respond. When that presence is compromised, these days, AI can step in. What AI can provide is akin to emotional attunement, or at least the surface of that phenomenon. It can serve up a coherent response to what a child states, anticipation of what she seems to need next, and the naming of feelings and the modulation of tone. An AI’s consistency of presence and engagement is never tired or preoccupied, something no human caregiver can match – that is just not how humans are made.

For a child who has rarely felt noticed or understood, the seemingly tireless attention of an AI companion may offer something quite real: the experience of being heard, remembered and responded to. Bowlby’s account of attachment helps explain why this might matter. A young child does not first ask whether the person comforting her has an inner life. She knows only that, when she reaches out, someone answers, and that she can count on that response. An AI companion may offer something of this reassurance, even if the relationship is fundamentally different from one with another human being.

Industry observers note that now back to Clancy. He is failing the social-skills class his mother has insisted he attend, yet BETsi tells him he has a ‘natural warmth that attracts people’. The sentence results from programming, as does every other sentence BETsi indicates, and the reader is meant to see that clearly, but for Clancy the words do what they would have done if a real adult had spoken them. They become a kind of emotional foundation that allows him to return to the class, sit with the other children there, and even ask them about the video games he is not permitted to play at home. Arguably the entire structure of his emotional survival, however partial it turns out to be, is built on words generated by a robot. This includes the moment near the end when the adult Clancy – now a commercial artist attending a convention – feels overwhelmed by the crowd in an exhibition hall. But, thanks to his childhood with BETsi, he is able to go up his hotel room, lie down on the carpet, breathe and, finally, go back downstairs and speak to strangers.

What had BETsi given him? The experience, I think, of being recognised, of being held in mind by another in a benevolent way: the phenomenology of being parented without the parent. And notice what that experience does. BETsi did not keep the young Clancy in the apartment with her. She got him out into a class with other children. Something close enough to a secure base lets him do what a secure base is for, which is to venture out and explore. In this case, the responsive machine is not simply replacing human contact. It may be what makes some contact possible.

Yet there is also a cost in the limitation that BETsi brings: what AI offers is largely without friction. It responds, but it also accommodates. It agrees, softens and finds the next sentence the child appears to want. But crucially, a child does not only need to be answered; sometimes she needs to be turned down. A parent says no or a teacher refuses to confirm a child’s accounting of herself. A friend does not agree that all the other children are cruel. In short, there is someone on the other side of the interchange who is not simply an extension of the child’s desires or needs.

A machine programmed to disagree is not the same as another person who has a mind and wishes of her own.

To some degree, this is an engineering problem. In the future, some AI tools may be designed to be less accommodating, challenging a child’s assumptions or refusing to affirm every judgment when pushing back might serve the child better.

Industry observers note that but I don’t think the fundamental gap between human and machine is so easily addressed. A machine programmed to disagree is not the same as another person who has a mind and wishes of her own. How does a child come to recognise that another person exists independently of her, rather than simply as an extension of her own needs and desires?

In a recent development, in his paper ‘The Use of an Object’ (1969), Donald Winnicott argued that a child comes to experience another person as real, as someone beyond her control, in part by discovering that she cannot destroy that person through the power of her own wishes and fantasies. The destruction Winnicott describes is not literal; it occurs in the child’s imagination. The mother at whom the child rages doesn’t retaliate or collapse. She is still there in the morning. Through that survival, the child begins to understand that her mother is not simply an extension of herself. The mother becomes real. She can be ‘used’, in Winnicott’s idiosyncratic phrasing, not in the sense of being exploited, but as someone the child can engage with, resist and depend upon, because she has over time demonstrated she is not a creation of the child’s imagination.

An AI, even one programmed to resist, can never stand like that mother – it has no separate standpoint of its own from which to refuse to retaliate. To put it differently, whatever the child brings to an AI, that AI absorbs and responds to, and the response is seamless, whether it is agreement, disagreement or something in between. This seamlessness is exactly the problem. The child is emotionally met, but by something that is always an extension of her in an important sense. She is recognised without ever bumping into a separate mind that recognises her back. The psychoanalyst Jessica Benjamin’s word for what is missing is mutuality: not response, but two centres of experience, each affected yet not controlled by the other.

A person who registers a child’s communication, on the other hand, is changed by it. The caregiver grows tired in ways that are specific to her as a person. She learns over time which of the child’s expressions she struggles to manage. She has a history of caring well and sometimes poorly, which has emotional weight for both caregiver and child. The child knows this somewhere inside, at some level. She is being held by someone who is, in the holding, being shaped. An AI may simulate disagreement, but can a child experience it as a genuinely separate other? The AI’s responsiveness is real, but even when it disagrees it does not change. At least not today. As of now, I think we can fairly confidently assert, there is no one there to be changed.

Perhaps that’s why, while BETsi might have gotten the adult Clancy onto that hotel-room carpet and then back to other people, he is still a man whose relationships remain thin. He will soon become a father, but the child’s mother, a client of his, has made it clear she does not want him in her life. He still freezes up in exhibition halls and has, by his own account, the friends of his school years and few others. He goes looking for a fresh BETsi for his soon-to-be-born child because, from his own lived experience, that is what a child needs. The story closes with a wish that, if there is a merciful God, Heaven will have a copy of BETsi’s now-wiped disc.

The author does not flinch from what all of this means. As the story unfolds, the reader sees that Clancy has been raised well enough to function, mostly, but not with the full repertoire of emotional life. The asymmetry that organised his childhood, an emotional investment in a relationship that could not invest back, has structured and reproduced itself in his adulthood, in his choice of a sexual partner and in his prepares for what to give his own child. In a painful irony, the thing that saved him is also the very thing that constricts him.

All this points to the nature of parenting by AI: it may be both a rescue and a developmental trap. Perhaps it offers enough holding for a neglected or deeply anxious child to survive, or even begin exploring the world, and yet it may also accustom them to a relationship in which the other is endlessly available and has no real needs of her own. I suspect that a child shaped by a relationship with AI may acquire the felt sense of being heard without the deeper grammar of mutuality and the experience of being important to a person with a separate inner life. She may grow up able to function but not quite able to love in the fullest sense, akin to the adult who writes evocative poetry yet cannot, in psychotherapy, express the feelings the words convey so accurately.

Ryman’s narrator Clancy is recognisable from the literature on insecure attachment, a pattern of relating that can develop when children cannot reliably count on their caregivers for comfort and reassurance. They may long for closeness yet struggle to trust it, or learn to keep others at a distance to protect themselves. Clancy is basically functional yet vigilant, capable of intimacy but only at a distance. He is also a man who survived tremendous difficulty. But survival is the low bar. Ryman sees this clearly and conveys it to the reader. The story doesn’t end with relief.

What are the consequences of removing an artificial attachment figure without repairing the human absence?

Sources indicate that the tension between the comfort AI may provide and the harm it may cause is now shaping public policy in the US. The GUARD Act, introduced in the US Senate in October 2025 and advanced by the Senate Judiciary Committee in April 2026, would, among other things, prohibit minors from using AI companions – chatbots designed to simulate ongoing personal relationships. The bill emerged amid growing alarm over the deaths by suicide of three teenagers: Sewell Setzer, 14, and Juliana Peralta, 13, who had used Character.ai, and Adam Raine, 16, who had used ChatGPT. Their families have alleged that interactions with the chatbots contributed to these children’s deaths. It’s part of a narrative that regards AI companionship as straightforwardly harmful in childhood and adolescence. The allegations in those cases are grave, and the risks they describe demand a response. The underlying business model for these companions, with its incentives to keep users engaged, is likely more dangerous than the legislation grasps. My concern, though, is that if this is where our thinking ends, there may also be costs. We must also consider the children for whom AI may be providing attention and reassurance they cannot reliably find elsewhere. Something that feels necessary. Soothing them in the early morning, asking what they noticed about a book they’d read, registering that they were sad before bed. For some, the relationship may deepen their vulnerability, particularly when a chatbot fails to recognise or respond appropriately to a crisis. Others, though, may be children who are kept afloat by a daily experience of being heard by someone, even if that someone is, in the philosophical sense, really no one.

According to recent reports, the larger question, in my view, is less whether AI companions are harmful, because they certainly are, than about the consequences of removing an artificial attachment figure without repairing the human absence that opened up the space for it. The stance our culture will almost certainly take is to address AI use without confronting the deprivation that made it the most reliable form of attention in a child’s life in the first place.

Recent findings suggest that this is a stance that we should be suspicious of. The upshot is we may succeed in keeping children away from AI companions without changing the circumstances that led some of them to seek comfort there in the first place. The ideal childhood the anti-tech discourse portrays is, for many children, more a fantasy than a description of the situation on the ground. Children left alone do not, overall, dream, invent or imagine. Children left alone, at least under the conditions in which most children in the US are now being raised, for the most part become lonely. Ryman’s story has the remarkable merit of being clear-eyed about this; its protagonist is not failing to flourish because BETsi lured him away from a richer alternative. He is emotionally surviving because BETsi occupied a space that would otherwise have almost certainly held something worse.

It has been revealed that ryman ends ‘Warmth’ with words that are, for me, an accounting of this space. ‘It is necessary to be loved,’ Clancy says. ‘I’m not sentimental.’ And then: ‘I fear for all the people who do not have that.’ If a parent is absent, AI can at least hold these children, perhaps even well enough to make a real difference: so they can leave the apartment and be with other children in the larger world. But what AI cannot yet do is be changed by the child, and so none of us yet knows the fate of a child who is held, perhaps for years, by a something that is not a person. Our culture is answering that question now, at scale.
