According to recent reports, mountain towns in winter are a culture in and of themselves, and if you don’t ski, don’t check snow forecasts, and have never once remarked “stoked” out loud, the whole scene kinda feels like showing up immensely overdressed to a casual happy hour. You already stick out and don’t know the lingo, and despite the casual style, ski resorts have a reputation for penny pinching, with lift tickets at high-end spots topping $200 per day. Then you walk into the coffee shop and the people in front of you in line keep talking about “skinning” and “slaying.” It’s no wonder the whole thing starts to feel a bit ominous.

I’ve lived in Colorado my whole life, and I’m here to tell you one thing: The code is easier to crack than it looks. Most of it comes down to dressing for the weather and taking satisfaction out of “type 2” fun. My advice to wary mountain travelers is to obtain three pieces of gear: a puffy jacket, a versatile backpack that works on the mountain and in town, and a good luggage set. Then, embark on a short vocabulary lesson, and you’ll be set up for success. Cotopaxi, the Salt Lake City outfitter whose striped puffies are a mountain town staple, makes a fitting guide to doing this the right way. Follow suit and you might even like it here.

Industry observers note that first rule: dress for technicality, not style. I went to college in Durango, Colorado, and over the course of my five years of “studying” the town was voted America’s worst-dressed multiple times. It’s all a ruse. You’ll certainly see someone at trivia night wearing sweatpants, but those sweatpants are fleece-lined, the hoodie is merino, and the puffy is 800-fill down. Sloppy may not be a good look, but cold is a mistake not worth making.

Industry observers note that layering is the whole trick. A base layer that wicks (wool or synthetic, never cotton), something warm in the middle and a puffy or shell on top. Mountain weather changes by the hour and by the elevation, so you want to be able to peel. Not to repeat myself, but avoid wearing that cotton/poly blend university logo sweatshirt – cotton is terrible when it’s wet, and there’s no surer sign you’re a tourist.

The puffy is where you spend the money, and it’s the one piece that works everywhere, from a 7 am sidewalk to a 7 pm bar. Cotopaxi’s Fuego Down Hooded Jacket ($300) is the brand’s flagship, and the recent redesign kept the part that matters: 800-fill responsibly sourced down. Fill power is warmth per ounce, and at that number the men’s jacket comes in around 13 ounces. The shell and lining are now 100 percent recycled nylon with a DWR finish, made without intentionally added PFAS chemicals. The whole thing stuffs into its own internal pocket for the flight home. Before you scoff at the price, the key to outdoor wear is to buy once and keep forever. You will not need to replace a well-made puffy jacket. Cotopaxi covers it with a lifetime warranty and repairs.

It also comes in colors, which is good because mountain towns are visually loud. A jacket in Carbon Stripes or Woods Stripes is in style every year. I’ve worn a Fuego hooded jacket for years, to the point that it’s rare I’m not wearing it between October and April. It’s been with me on mountain peaks in Kyrgyzstan and on the patio at the brewery a few blocks from my house, and I feel just as comfortable in either setting.

Checked luggage is the one place a city dweller can out-gear the locals, since most of them haven’t flown with a suitcase in years. Mountain town airports are small and the carousels are slow. Not the moment for a bag with a wobbly wheel.

There are some common phrases you’ll encounter on your trip. Most are far more innocent and basic than they hit the first time you hear them.

I’ll never forget the time back in college when I complained to a buddy about a long lift line or something of the sort, groaning that I wanted people to just go home. His response set the course for how I view mountain towns to this day: the visitors basically sign our paychecks. None of this would be here without them.

Sources indicate that that knowledge is key to settling into mountain life. Ski towns are friendly places where travelers come from all over to have a good time. Say hi to people on the sidewalk. Tip in cash. Newcomers tend to assume the town exists for the mountain. It exists for the people who live there, and most of them do plenty besides ski. Just don’t bring up real estate prices with anyone who was born there.
