In 1981, Kurt Vonnegut published Palm Sunday, a grab bag of speeches, essays, letters, and odds and ends that he subtitled An Autobiographical Collage, and partway through it he did something few novelists would risk in print: he gave himself a report card. Mental Floss has run the full list of grades before, in a piece about one of the two books at the top of it. The list alone doesn't show the rule he graded by or why one novel landed at the bottom, and it stops before he did.

According to recent reports, vonnegut set out the rules in a short preamble. "I have graded my separate works from A to D," he wrote. "The grades I hand out to myself do not place me in literary history. I am comparing myself with myself." That, he explained, was how he could give himself an A-plus for Cat's Cradle while knowing "that there was a writer named William Shakespeare." The list runs in order of publication, so readers could "plot my rise and fall on graph paper, if you like.".

It has been revealed that the rise came first. Player Piano, his 1952 debut, got a B. The Sirens of Titan, Mother Night, and God Bless You, Mr. Rosewater each got an A, and Cat's Cradle (1963) and Slaughterhouse-Five (1969) got the only two A-pluses. The fall came after that: a B-minus for the story collection Welcome to the Monkey House, a C for Breakfast of Champions, and another C for the essay collection Wampeters, Foma & Granfalloons. Jailbird, his newest novel, pulled him back up to an A in 1979, and Palm Sunday earned a C, meaning the book you were holding while you read the grades was graded below Player Piano.

According to recent reports, two works got a D. One was his play Happy Birthday, Wanda June, and the other was Slapstick, the 1976 novel about a pair of giant, freakish twins who are geniuses together and dull apart. It was also the one he'd tied most closely to his own life: "This is the closest I will ever come to writing an autobiography," its prologue begins, and the twins were modeled on Vonnegut and his sister Alice, who died of cancer in 1958.

Recent findings suggest that the critics hated it. Greil Marcus, reviewing it for Rolling Stone in October 1976, called the novel "cute, contrived, demeaning, devoid of real feeling," and the reviews in The Upcoming York Times and The Village Voice were just as rough. When WNYC's Walter James Miller sat down with Vonnegut that month, he invited the author to answer them. Five years later, Vonnegut more or less agreed with them on paper, though by Open Culture's account he later told Charlie Rose he'd been too hard on the book.
