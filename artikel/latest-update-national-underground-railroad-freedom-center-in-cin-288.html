The National Underground Railroad Freedom Center, located in Cincinnati along the banks of the Ohio River makes a solemn statement about the lives that were lived enslaved in the early history of the United States, and the many heroic men and women who played important roles in the Underground Railroad movement, which helped to bring an end to African slavery in the United States. Referred to as a "museum of conscience", The Freedom Center is dedicated to exploring the struggle to abolish human enslavement. During the era when the Underground Railroad was active, Cincinnati was a major gateway across the Ohio River where freedom seekers escaped from southern slave states to the northern states or Canada, making the Center's location rather significant.

Having opened its doors in 2004, the Freedom Center uses immersive exhibits across multiple floors to convey an account that is simultaneously rich in the cultural traditions of the enslaved, as well as steeped in the violence and tragedies that were endured. The histories are told through a combination of permanent and traveling exhibits that utilize storytelling, film, artwork, hands-on activities and more.

Industry observers note that perhaps one of the most impressive and prominent displays at the center is an authentic, 19th-century reconstructed slave pen that was obtained from a farm in Kentucky. It stands as America's only remaining, pristinely preserved slave pen and stands as a stark reminder of the atrocious conditions that were endured.

According to recent reports, the pen, originally built somewhere around 1830 by a wealthy Kentucky farmer, runaway slave catcher, and slave trader, was used as a holding facility for captured and purchased enslaved men, women and children. It was discovered, quite by accident in 1998 when a farmer, who was preparing to demolish an old barn on his property, discovered that the barn had been built up around this old pen, thus protecting and preserving (and hiding) the slave pen for more than 100 years.

During its unfortunate years of use, enslaved people would be kept in the pen, often for months, until the slave trader accumulated a large enough "stock" to march them further south to be sold. These forced marches would often cover upwards of 750 miles. Conditions in these pens would be brutal, offering no sanitation, lighting or warmth. As many as 65 enslaved people would often be housed in this small pen. Also very moving is the memorial room dedicated to the Transatlantic Slave Trade, as well as the Freedom's Eternal Flame Terrace which overlooks the Ohio River and the city skyline.

The National Underground Railroad Freedom Center is a beautiful and meaningful tribute to the human right for freedom, and to the brave men, women and children who stood up to the inhuman treatment of themselves and of others; and to those who continue to fight for the cause of freedom through out the world today.

This is a very moving, sobering and comprehensive experience. Initiative on spending at least 3-4 hours.

Admission is $21.50 for adults ($20 on Wed and Thursdays); lower rates for seniors and children. Kids under 3 are free.

Admission is free certain days of the year, usually 1 Sunday every month as well as MLK Day and Juneteenth.

Last entry is at 4:30pm. There is a gift shop on site. Parking can be tricky with a lot of one-way streets and parking garages in the area. There is pretty plentiful parking, if you can navigate all the one way streets to get to the parking area you desire.
