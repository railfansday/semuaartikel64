Industry observers note that some casual filmgoers might say that Martin Scorsese's filmography primarily contains gangster movies, but that overlooks just how varied his 50-plus-year career actually has been. Sure, his films often return to familiar themes of guilt, faith, masculinity, and people destroying themselves in pursuit of something they believe will make them whole.Â.

But much of that comes from his upbringing in Manhattan, where chronic asthma kept him indoors watching movies while the surrounding neighborhood exposed him to street violence and the complicated world of Italian-American identity.Â.

His career has moved from the raw New York stories of the 1970s to the crime epics of the '90s, and he has also made increasingly ambitious historical and spiritual explorations in the decades since.Â.

For a filmmaker so associated with restless energy, his later work has often been focused on looking backward, and along those lines, confronting mortality and questioning the people and things he once found fascinating.

Scorsese has dozens of acclaimed movies, and these are his ten highest-rated ones on Rotten Tomatoes. This list focuses on his narrative features, so you won't find documentaries like Rolling Thunder Revue and The Last Waltz on here.Â.

His obsession with a talk-show host gradually crosses beyond every type of acceptable fan behavior, and this makes the film a great examination of celebrity culture and parasocial relationships that is as relevant now as ever.Â.

Pupkin doesn't really see himself as someone doing anything wrong, and considers his increasingly outrageous behavior to be simply what it takes to get his big break.Â The film also takes some sharp swings at the entertainment industry's obsession with fame and the people willing to exploit it.

Mean Streets may occasionally feel like a slow burn, especially for viewers expecting the pacing found in other Scorsese crime flicks, but the movie is a very rewarding experience.Â.

Set in New York's Little Italy, the film follows Charlie (played by Harvey Keitel), a small-time criminal trying to balance his life. His biggest problem is Johnny Boy (Robert De Niro), a friend whose inability to control himself repeatedly brings trouble to everyone around him.

Unlike the polished and almost grand crime worlds of his later gangster classics, Mean Streets demonstrates organized crime at its lowest level, focusing on cramped apartments, fights over petty debts, and people who aren't half as powerful as they imagine themselves to be.Â.

The movie also dedicates a pretty good portion of the film's soundtrack to classic rock, pop, and soul music, using songs by The Rolling Stones and The Ronettes to express the characters' psychology and the energy of their surroundings.
