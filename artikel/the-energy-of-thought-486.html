The energy of thought

🌐 Source: Aeon • 📅 27 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 16 min read (3157 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Making sense of the mind is, in part, a matter of modelling, and philosophers and scientists have created an astonishing number of models.
  • However, nearly all of these models neglect something essential to how the human mind functions: metabolism.
  • So what happens if we take brain metabolism seriously?
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Making sense of the mind is, in part, a matter of modelling, and philosophers and scientists have created an astonishing number of models. Some liken the mind to a computer. Some emphasise symbols and ‘mentalese’, a hypothesised ‘language of thought’. Some start from the brain and its physical, neural basis. These models aren’t necessarily in conflict: for instance, the ‘working memory’ model proposed by the psychologists Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch may complement ‘predictive processing’ models of vision or attention theorised by the computational neuroscientists Rajesh Rao, Dana Ballard and Karl Friston. The domain is crowded, and still growing.

However, nearly all of these models neglect something essential to how the human mind functions: metabolism. They describe what the mind computes, or how its neurons signal, but not the metabolic work by which the brain acquires energy, maintains homeostasis, and disposes of waste. Without taking metabolism into consideration, our models are in danger of becoming untethered from real-world constraints, turning the ancient goal of understanding the mind into a wild goose chase.

So what happens if we take brain metabolism seriously?

‘Metabolism’ refers to activities that all biological systems, the brain included, carry out in order to stay alive. It includes energy use, but also maintenance, regulation and waste removal. Metabolism informs models of how our muscles work when we run, how our lungs work when we breathe, and how our immune system works when we get sick. Since the human mind has something to do with the brain, it stands to reason that metabolism should inform models of how our brain works when we think. And, indeed, most scholars nowadays tend to accept that the brain is vital to understanding the mind, even if they leave it out of their models.

Recent findings suggest that the brain is a quirky organ, metabolically speaking. It consumes a lot of energy compared with other parts of the human body. Though it makes up around 2 per cent of an adult’s body mass, it consumes about 20 per cent of its calories. Yet it is impressively efficient. The brain on average operates at about 20 watts – less than an incandescent lightbulb. A ‘lightbulb moment’, in other words, probably consumes less energy than an actual lightbulb.

According to one study by William Levy and Victoria Calvert of the University of Virginia, the electrical signals (or ‘spikes’) that send information use only about 3.5 watts. Building and strengthening synapses, which is how information is stored, uses about 1.3 watts. It is not entirely understood how the brain can do so much with so little, but part of the answer is evolution: organisms with more efficient brains had a selective advantage for searching for food and avoiding predators. The human mind operates under tremendous metabolic constraints.

Focusing on metabolism might seem to fly in the face of much philosophical analysis of the mind. Many philosophers set the brain aside when building their theories, freeing the mind from its substratum. One reason is the widespread acceptance of multiple realisability, a view first set out by the philosopher Hilary Putnam in the 1960s, which holds that the same mental states can be realised by different neural states. What determines a mental state is what it does, not what it’s made of. This is how an octopus could still feel pain, even if its brain is unlike ours – it’s the state that matters, not the physical substratum. Some philosophers go further, adopting John Haugeland’s notion of ‘medium independence’: if mental states are computations – a mainstream view in cognitive science – then these states are independent of the specific medium in which they are embodied. Mental states are defined by formal rules, and these same rules can be implemented in different media. The familiar analogy is the computer: the same software (the mind) can be run on different hardware (the brain). Both views reject the idea that there is a one-to-one match between the mind and the brain. They suggest that the mind can be fruitfully studied on its own terms, making metabolism irrelevant to modelling.

Yet even brain-friendly philosophers have largely disregarded brain metabolism. Patricia Churchland, who urged philosophers to learn neuroscience, co-wrote a 1988 paper that defined the aim of computational neuroscience as explaining how the brain’s signals are used ‘to represent and process information’. But the chemistry that maintains those signals was lost in this framing. The philosopher Carl Craver and colleagues stay close to the biological ground when they argue that mechanisms are ‘entities and activities organised such that they are productive of regular changes’, for example neurotransmitters binding to receptors. But here too the energy that makes those activities possible figures as a background condition rather than as part of the explanation.

Sources indicate that we argue that metabolism constrains which models of the mind are viable. But that does not require the mind to be uniquely realised by the brain: metabolic constraints narrow the options without settling on a single one. Consider the example of memory storage. It could occur via different brain processes, or in nonbiological media like silicon-based digital computers. Whatever the medium, all these systems will be constrained by energy use, maintenance and waste removal. A model of memory is not viable if it requires a system to do more than its metabolism can support, and studying metabolism is how we find out where that limit lies.

So the metabolic constraints on physical systems like brains cannot be ignored when modelling the mind. This means rethinking our stance towards views like multiple realisability and medium independence. Rather than taking them to be firm metaphysical commitments, we should think of them as working hypotheses: great starting points, but open to revision after newly introduced research.

Recently, the philosophers Mazviita Chirimuuta, Corey Maley and Rosa Cao have each argued that information-processing in brains depends on signal amplitudes, metabolic molecules and energy supply. This suggests a tight link between mind and metabolism, but it does not decisively rule out multiple realisability or medium independence. Instead, it shows that studying brain metabolism can test how much these hypotheses help us understand the mind. Looking at metabolism is therefore important, even if future science finds out that there is no one-to-one relation between mind and brain, that minds can exist in silicon, or that body and environment are essential for cognition.

The past 25 years have seen great advances in our understanding of brain metabolism, providing us with a wealth of material for studying metabolic constraints on the mind. We now have detailed energy budgets: the brain devotes about 16 per cent of its energy to generating action potentials, 44 per cent to transmitting these signals chemically at the synapse, and 40 per cent to maintaining and repairing neuron cells. This means that only 60 per cent of brain energy is for signalling, while the rest is spent keeping the neural machinery from falling apart. According to a 2015 paper by the neuroscientists Elisabeth Engl and David Attwell, energy for maintenance and repair ‘must contribute to limiting the cognitive abilities of the brain because the total energy use of the brain constrains the temporal rate at which information can be encoded and processed.’ But budgets like this are too coarse on their own to serve as constraints, especially when modelling individual cognitive capacities. Numerical costs of information-processing, by contrast, are more specific. According to one study by the theoretical neuroscientist Jan Karbowski and colleagues, storing one bit of information in the synapse costs about 500,000 ATP (the ‘fuel’ of the cell).

Return to the example of memory. How can metabolism help us assess whether a model of memory is plausible? The dominant theoretical framework in neuroscience is that memory is stored in synapses: the junctions where signals pass from one neuron another. When a new memory is formed, the synapses change, affecting the signals that are transmitted. One such change is known as ‘Hebbian learning’, named for the neuropsychologist Donald Hebb, whose work is summed up in the slogan (not his) ‘neurons that fire together, wire together’.

Models are useful when they solve a theory’s problems, and the synaptic theory has two major ones. The first, often called interference, is that synapses that change easily are good at storing fresh memories but bad at keeping old ones. Almost any change in neural firing alters a synapse’s molecular makeup slightly. But then how do new memories keep from overwriting old ones? The second problem is called turnover. Molecules that make up a synapse are replaced within days, yet some memories last a lifetime. In order to stay alive, neurons must constantly rebuild their decaying parts. A neuron is like Otto Neurath’s ship, rebuilt plank by plank at sea. How does information survive in a system whose every part is being replaced?

Recent findings suggest that in 2016, Stefano Fusi and Marcus Benna published a mathematical model to solve the interference problem. It asks us to imagine the synaptic memory process as a set of beakers – each standing for a chemical process at the synapse – filled with liquid and connected by tubes. The level in the first beaker is the synapse’s strength: adding liquid strengthens it, and removing liquid weakens it. ‘Over time,’ Benna explained in an interview, ‘the resulting flow of liquid will diffuse across the other beakers, corresponding to the long-term storage of memories.’ Because those deeper beakers fill and drain more slowly, they hold a trace of past changes and keep drawing the first beaker back towards its old level. New memories form rapidly; old ones fade only slowly, as the levels even out.

According to recent reports, however, metabolic constraints render this solution implausible. The beaker model describes a system in which biochemical processes in the synapse even out over time: liquid can flow one way or the other, but it ultimately reaches equilibrium. In biology, settling into equilibrium means death. As the physicist Erwin Schrödinger put it: ‘Living matter evades the decay to equilibrium.’ Neurons must take in and expel ions and molecules to stay alive. A neuron that reaches equilibrium loses its organisation and can no longer perform functions such as storing memories at the synapse. Treating metabolism as a constraint shows us that a linear beaker model does not solve the problem of forming newly introduced synaptic memories and keeping old ones. This consequence is easy to miss if modellers do not consider the relevance of metabolism.

In a recent development, metabolism itself, though, points to a better model. Karbowski has developed a model of synaptic memory in which metabolism takes centre stage. Because living systems are never at equilibrium, he builds his model with the mathematics of nonequilibrium thermodynamics. Unlike the beaker model, this framework describes the resting state of the synapse as not being in equilibrium with its environment, and thus capable of storing memories.

Holding on to old memories costs so little that the synapse can afford to keep them while laying down new ones.

Industry observers note that the model links biochemical processes at the synapse in cycles rather than in linear sequences (as in the beaker model). ATP powers transitions between states in the cycle, so metabolism is built into the model directly. These transitions represent how the synapse strengthens or weakens to store or erase memories. Because the state changes in the cycle have different rates, they never reach equilibrium. The synapse can form latest memories and keep them over time without dying.

Recent findings suggest that karbowski then combines the cyclic model with the numerical costs of information-processing (such as the ATP cost per bit of memory storage). His calculations show that encoding new memories is energetically cheap, and so is, under certain conditions, storing them for a long time. That may explain why interference doesn’t erase long-lasting memories: holding on to them costs so little that the synapse can afford to keep old memories while laying down new ones. The same picture suggests an answer to the turnover problem: if a memory is held in a state that the synapse actively maintains, rather than in any particular molecules, it can outlast the molecules themselves, much as Neurath’s ship stays afloat precisely because it is constantly being rebuilt.

Recent findings suggest that A second example of how metabolism can inform models comes from research on mental effort. When you start work in the morning, you feel fresh. But as the day grinds on and you’re writing your 700th email and attending your fifth meeting, tasks that were easy become gruelling. This is the cost of mental effort. How can mental effort be modelled? One possibility is the ego depletion model, developed by the psychologist Roy Baumeister and colleagues. It posits that performing effortful tasks depends on a finite resource: glucose in the brain. The decrease in performance is explained by the decrease of the resource.

But the model has been challenged on empirical grounds, such as difficulties with replicating its findings. As a result, researchers such as Robert Kurzban and colleagues have turned towards computational models of mental effort, in which the brain calculates the benefits and costs of staying on task versus switching to another activity. Proponents conclude that computational models are superior, and that metabolism is not relevant to model mental effort.

Yet the opposition between resource models such as ego depletion and computational models of mental effort is false. Ego depletion was undone partly by metabolic evidence: the brain’s glucose use barely changes during demanding tasks, far too little for an afternoon of self-control to run it down. That is metabolism doing just what we have described, ruling out a model the brain could not run. And computational models face metabolic questions of their own, because weighing the costs and benefits of staying on task is itself work that must be paid for. Neuroscientists such as Amitai Shenhav, Matthew Botvinick and Clay Holroyd believe this computational process takes place in a brain area known as the anterior cingulate cortex. We can use evidence about available energy to assess whether models of this process are feasible.

It has been revealed that some researchers already integrate metabolism into their models of mental effort. Alexandre Zénon and colleagues, for instance, posit that effortful tasks require the brain to process more information and therefore use slightly more energy. Since its energy budget has a hard upper limit, the brain needs to spend less energy on other tasks. Zénon and his team explicitly link metabolism to our subjective feeling of effort, which might reflect how fast metabolic changes build up during demanding tasks. Fatigue, in turn, might reflect our expectation that these changes will continue.

In a recent development, likewise, Holroyd’s ‘controllosphere’ model starts from the theory of how the brain controls behaviour and draws on empirical findings that sustained mental effort produces waste in neurons. On this model, the feeling of effort is a signal to the brain to stop tasks that require high control, so that waste can be cleared before causing damage. Research on mental effort highlights that including metabolism into models of the mind is not a mere theoretical possibility. It is already a practical reality.

In a recent development, attention to metabolism and energy use can improve more than just our knowledge of biological organisms. It can also help our understanding of artificial neural networks at the heart of dramatic advances in artificial intelligence. While the speed of development is new, the link between AI and the brain is old, going back to the first neural network model in 1943, from pioneers such as Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts, or ‘the perceptron’ of Frank Rosenblatt in 1958. Recent advances like LLMs (large language models) reignited existential questions about whether AI is really intelligent, or even conscious. Metabolism and energy use should inform how we approach these questions.

Recent findings suggest that the relationship between AI and energy use is largely uncharted territory, and a metabolically informed perspective suggests where to start looking. Hyperscale data centres use a lot of energy, far beyond the 20 watts the human brain runs on. Yet in philosophy and cognitive science, views like medium independence lead scholars to think of AI in abstract, formal terms: while the physical basis of an AI system may vary, the computation, so the thought goes, is the same. This focus on abstract computation leads philosophers such as Luke Kersten and Leonard Dung to argue that AI systems could, in principle, duplicate human minds. But, in brains, energy constraints help determine which computations are performed in the first place. If the same holds for artificial systems, then whether a chip and a brain are running ‘the same’ computation cannot simply be assumed. It has to be found out.

Unlike brains, AI systems are things we design, so we can change how they use energy and see what follows.

Industry observers note that our research belongs to a growing discussion of energy use and its role in modelling both brains and AI systems. For example, Cao and the neuroscientist Daniel Yamins have studied how AI models implement a highly abstract version of the mechanism that brains use to respond to novel visual stimuli. Cao and Yamins recognise that brains and engineered AI face different energy constraints, yet they consider energetics only abstractly, in terms of efficiency and optimisation. We propose instead to rethink energy use in AI from the bottom up. How an AI system uses energy is a modelling problem, which depends on scale, training data and details of grid infrastructure, just as the brain’s energy use depends on the details of its metabolism. This crucial parallel between studying brains and AI systems is missed when we focus on task optimisation alone. Modelling AI energy use informs how and when energetic differences lead to computational, perhaps even cognitive, differences.

Unlike brains, AI systems are things we design, so we can change how they use energy and see what follows. AI energy use is a practical problem that the study of metabolism can help tackle. Cognitive scientists such as Megan Peters are already looking to the brain to improve energy efficiency in AI. These projects highlight the practical benefits that come from studying brain metabolism for engineering projects like AI model building. They also have theoretical benefits: projects that include energy use serve as tests of philosophical positions like medium independence.

When it comes to figuring out how to model minds, it is time that researchers take a ‘metabolic turn’, to quote our colleague Patrick McGivern. Taking seriously how systems use energy and regulate themselves gives us upcoming ways of addressing the toughest questions that we face in making sense of the mind.
