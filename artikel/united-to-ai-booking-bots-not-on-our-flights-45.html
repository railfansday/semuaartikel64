United to AI Booking Bots: Not on Our Flights.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

By now, even travelers who don’t use AI frequently know that AI’s capabilities are robust. AI can help you find cheap flights, build your itinerary, recommend hotels, and figure out whether a three-hour layover is enough time to leave the airport. But if you’re hoping to hand over your credit card information and let an AI assistant book your next vacation, United Airlines has some bad news.

It has been revealed that on November 11, United announced that it’s restricting unauthorized third-party AI agents from booking flights and managing reservations on behalf of travelers. The restrictions apply to the airline’s website and app, as well as bookings made through travel agencies and third-party platforms like Expedia and Priceline.

According to recent reports, united says the move is meant to protect travelers from security risks and unauthorized bookings, while also keeping the airline in compliance with federal regulations. But there may be another reason United doesn’t want AI handling bookings: losing control over the booking process.

United notes giving third-party AI assistants access to customer accounts could expose sensitive information and lead to surprise purchases, unauthorized changes to reservations, and bots using MileagePlus miles without a user’s knowledge.

Recent findings suggest that there are also regulatory concerns. During check-in, passengers must acknowledge safety information about prohibited items in their luggage, including explosives. An AI agent could potentially click through that step without actually knowing what’s inside someone’s bag. United notes allowing AI to handle these acknowledgments could violate FAA and Department of Transportation (DOT) regulations. The airline is updating its booking, ticketing, and distribution policies to restrict these automated interactions, including those carried out indirectly through travel agencies and aggregators.

Recent findings suggest that yes. There’s an important distinction between using AI to research flights and allowing an AI agent to book them.

In a recent development, travelers can still use AI tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and any others to suggest destinations, compare potential routes, research fares, or put together itineraries that include United flights. You can even pass those tasks off to an AI agent. But under the new rule, those agents can’t finalize the purchase or modify an existing purchase. So ChatGPT can still find you the best price for the best flight from San Francisco to Tokyo, but you’ll have to book it yourself. This applies to all “unauthorized AI agents” — meaning AI agents not authorized by the airline.

Travelers can also still use existing flight-search platforms that sync with United, like Google Flights and Kayak, which allow you to book through approved channels.

It has been revealed that southwest Airlines is going in the opposite direction of United. In October, it launched ChatGPT integration that gives the AI platform direct, accurate access to Southwest’s flight inventory, so it has access to the best flight data. The airline says it plans to expand its AI-powered booking capabilities in the future, and while it doesn’t have a statement on whether AI agents are right now allowed to book flights, there are plenty of accounts online of travelers using it for that task.

United hasn’t ruled out working with AI booking tools forever, and notes it will continue evaluating the technology as AI assistants evolve.

United indicates its restrictions are about protecting customers, but airline analysts have suggested there may be another motivation: staying in control of the booking process.

Aviation analyst Henry Harteveldt told CBS News that United likely wants to avoid introducing a powerful and theoretically unbiased middleman between the airline and its customers.

It has been revealed that AI booking agents could eventually compare fares across dozens of airlines, find the cheapest options, and complete purchases without the need for travelers to visit an airline website or even offer input into what airline they fly. That could make booking easier for consumers, but it would also give airlines less control over how their product is presented and sold to customers. There could also be financial considerations, as AI agents use the airline’s hosted reservation systems, and a huge increase in searches and bandwidth could increase their operational costs.

Industry observers note that as of November 2026, United hasn’t given any reason for the change other than security concerns, which aren’t unfounded. AI agents aren’t infallible, and giving them access to sensitive personal information comes with risks. They can misunderstand instructions or, theoretically, take actions you never explicitly authorized. There’s also the question of how securely your information is stored and who else might gain access to it now or in the future, legally or otherwise. Generally, the less access an AI agent has to your credit cards, bank accounts, and other financial information, the less damage it can do if something goes wrong. And with AI researchers and security experts increasingly warning about the risks of giving AI systems too much control, there’s a reasonable argument for keeping humans in charge of decisions involving their money and personal information.
