Neither Native Americans nor the Pilgrims could have predicted that this one-off meal, celebrating an abundant harvest and a brief alliance, would be printed in history books as the first Thanksgiving. The Wampanoag, having already had contact with Europeans before the Pilgrims arrived (contact that resulted in diseases and the enslavement of some tribe members like Squanto), spoke English by the time of the feast.

To give thanks for a bountiful harvest and their first peace treaty, signed earlier that year (despite hesitation from tribal leaders), both groups sat down for a meal together, made possible by the hunting and planting techniques the Wampanoag shared with the Pilgrims, which greatly alleviated food shortages.Â.

According to historical records cited by Smithsonian Magazine, the feast lasted for three days.

"Many of the Indians coming amongst us, and among the rest their greatest king Massasoit, with some 90 men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted," English leader Edward Winslow wrote of the occasion.Â.

Sources indicate that although the gathering itself was friendly, the alliance did not last. Just 54 years after the first Thanksgiving, the treaty dissolved, sparking King Philip's War (led by Massasoit's son), in which the English defeated the Native Americans. The battle resulted in mass casualties, and the Wampanoag lost their land and freedoms.

Sources indicate that both settlers and Native American tribes held feasts to commemorate significant events, but this Thanksgiving feast did not become an annual tradition. In fact, it all but faded from memory until Abraham Lincoln proclaimed it a national holiday during the Civil War in 1863.
